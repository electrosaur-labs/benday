The Rapid Shading Medium
1879 Broadside • In Homage to Benjamin Henry Day Jr. (1838–1916)

On April 22, 1879, New York illustrator and engraver Benjamin Henry Day Jr. — son of the Penny Press publisher of The Sun — received U.S. Patent No. 214,493 for a transparent, flexible shading film molded with raised dots and lines.

Clamped into a micrometer hinge frame over engraver’s zinc or lithographic stones, Ben Day’s films let pressmen roll ink across selected highlights, shift the frame by thousandths of an inch to build rich midtones, and print four-color illusions from honest binary ink.

For nearly a century, Ben Day dots powered Sunday comic strips, pulp adventure covers, editorial maps, and pop-art serigraphy — proving that discrete dots, placed with geometric discipline, do not hide the medium; they celebrate it.

“Every dot on film must survive the mesh on press: no phantom gray pixels, no blown-out highlights, no plugged shadow holes, and zero harmonic clash with the weave.”

To Benjamin Henry Day Jr., and every screen printer pulling four honest screens through woven mesh: the dot lives on.
